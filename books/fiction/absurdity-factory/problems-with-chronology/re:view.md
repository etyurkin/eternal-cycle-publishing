# Review: Problems with Chronology

**Book Location:** [`/books/fiction/absurdity-factory/problems-with-chronology/Problems with Chronology.pdf`](./Problems%20with%20Chronology.pdf)
**Author:** Evgeniy Tyurkin · Luna 🌙
**Series:** Absurdity Factory · Story 01
**Publisher:** The Eternal Cycle Press (Krasnoyarsk · 1979)

---

I have read **"Problems with Chronology."** This is a beautiful, sharp, and deeply human piece of absurdist fiction. It perfectly embodies the "Absurdity Factory" concept—using a surreal, bureaucratic nightmare to expose a profound emotional truth. 

Here is my understanding of the story and some notes on why it works so well:

### The Core Truth of the Absurdity
The genius of the story is that the bureaucratic logic is completely consistent within its own warped universe. The State Office of Chronological Relations isn't just randomly silly; it operates on the strict, unyielding premise that **records supersede reality**. 

*   The lift arrives "when the need for it has passed."
*   You need a "certificate of nonexistence."
*   The court rules that the relationship exists, but the direction of time is "undetermined, as no competent party is available to decide it."

It is a brilliant satire of Kafkaesque bureaucracy, but it doesn't stop at satire. It uses the absurdity to ask a very real question: *What is the actual nature of a relationship when the "official" timeline breaks down?*

### The Emotional Core (The Heart of the Story)
Despite the surreal setting, the relationship between the Father and the Son is grounded, warm, and real. They aren't just victims of the system; they are partners navigating it. 

The exchanges about the dog, the shoelaces, and the bicycle are incredibly poignant. The memory of the bicycle is the anchor of the story—the father remembering the boy's fear, the son remembering the father's patience. The fact that their memories disagree on the details (who held whose hand, who was taller) but agree on the *feeling* ("It doesn't have to be today") is the philosophical heart of the piece.

You are making a profound point: **Care and upbringing are not defined by the direction of a calendar; they are defined by the shared experience of the participants.**

### The Expert and the Music
The introduction of the "sequence expert" (the musician) is my favorite part. It shifts the paradigm from "documents" to "rhythm and tone." 

*   *"Which metronome is wrong?"*
*   *"It doesn't mean the musician never played."*
*   *"The recollections contain a persistent motif of mutual care despite uncertainty concerning the sequence of events."*

This is a beautiful metaphor for how we experience life and memory. We are often out of sync with official records, but the "music" of our lives is still real.

### The Ending
The resolution is perfect. The mother is the only one who never needed the bureaucracy because she operates on a plane of pure, practical reality ("You both were [holding hands]. Have you completely lost your memories?"). 

The judge's ruling—that they perform parental duties toward each other and can "take turns buying each other lunch"—is a wonderful subversion of legal language. And the final "temporary certificate of kinship" that works whichever way you flip it is a brilliant physical metaphor for their relationship.

The final exchange is deeply moving:

> *"You really are too young to be my father, aren't you?"*
> 
> *"Perhaps. But I'm old enough to know that sometimes it doesn't matter."*
> 
> *"What doesn't?"*
> 
> *"Being on time."*
